OUTCOME B — this page + Synchronous Machines + Induction Motors together make up everything examined under Outcome B (your Assessment B-1 / B-2 style tests): AC-machine theory applied to real driven systems — pumps, scrapers, gearboxes, fuse sizing, and VSDs.

⚙️ Outcome B: AC Systems & Mechanics

Induction-motor-driven pumps, scrapers and gearboxes, hydraulic power, VSD sizing, and fuse/protection rating — exactly the style of your B-1 and B-2 assessments.

1. Same mechanics, AC machine on the front end

The gearbox and power-flow rules are identical to Outcome A — the only difference is that the prime mover is now an induction motor, so its own speed depends on slip (n = (1−s)ns), and its electrical input depends on power factor as well as efficiency:

\[ P_{\text{load}}=T_{\text{load}}\omega_{\text{load}} \qquad P_{\text{motor shaft}}=\frac{P_{\text{load}}}{\eta_{\text{gearbox}}} \qquad P_{\text{electrical}}=\frac{P_{\text{motor shaft}}}{\eta_{\text{motor}}} \]

Once you have the required electrical input power, 3-phase line current follows from:

\[ P=\sqrt{3}\,V_LI_L\cos\phi \]
Applies to both wye and delta using line voltage \(V_L\).
Whether the motor itself is star or delta connected doesn't change this line-current formula — VL is always line-to-line voltage. Star/delta only matters if you need the phase current or phase voltage specifically.

2. Hydraulic power (pumping systems)

For a pump lifting water (or any fluid) through a height, the useful ("hydraulic") power is just the rate of doing work against gravity:

\[ P_{\text{hydraulic}}=\frac{\rho Vgh}{t} \]
For water, \(\rho \approx 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\).

where V = volume moved (m³), h = height lifted (m), t = time taken (s). This hydraulic power is the pump's useful output — work backward through the pump efficiency, then the gearbox efficiency, then the motor efficiency to find the required electrical input, exactly as in the efficiency-chain method from Outcome A.

3. Worked example — scraper mechanism (gearbox + induction motor)

Worked example

A squirrel-cage induction machine (3 pole pairs = 6 poles, delta, 380 V, 86% efficient) drives a scraper through a 210:15 reduction gearbox (67% efficient). At maximum load the slip is 3% and power factor is 0.87. The scraper torque is 200 N·m. Find the total electrical input power to the machine.

  1. Synchronous speed: ns = 120(50)/6 = 1000 rpm. Motor speed at 3% slip: n = 970 rpm.
  2. Gearbox ratio = 210/15 = 14. Scraper (output) speed = 970/14 ≈ 69.3 rpm → ω ≈ 7.26 rad/s.
  3. Scraper (load) power = T×ω = 200×7.26 ≈ 1451 W.
  4. Motor shaft power = load power ÷ gearbox efficiency = 1451/0.67 ≈ 2167 W.
  5. Electrical input power = motor shaft power ÷ motor efficiency = 2167/0.86 ≈ 2519 W ≈ 2.52 kW.

Notice the power factor (0.87) wasn't actually needed to find the input power — it would matter if the question instead asked for line current.

4. Worked example — pump + fuse sizing

Worked example

A 6-pole, 400 V, wye-connected induction machine drives a pump through a 105:3 reduction gearbox (56% efficient). 100 m³ of water must be lifted 15 m in 1 hour. Pump efficiency 70%, motor efficiency 80%, motor power factor 0.85. Design the fuse rating, allowing 20% overrating for start-up.

  1. Hydraulic power: P = (1000×100×9.81×15)/3600 ≈ 4088 W.
  2. Pump shaft input = hydraulic power ÷ pump efficiency = 4088/0.70 ≈ 5839 W.
  3. Motor shaft output = pump shaft input ÷ gearbox efficiency = 5839/0.56 ≈ 10 427 W.
  4. Motor electrical input = motor shaft output ÷ motor efficiency = 10427/0.80 ≈ 13 034 W.
  5. Line current: IL = P/(√3·VL·pf) = 13034/(1.732×400×0.85) ≈ 22.1 A.
  6. Fuse rating (with 20% margin) = 22.1×1.2 ≈ 26.6 A.

5. VSDs and induction machine control

A Variable Speed Drive (VSD/inverter) sits between the mains and the motor, itself introducing an extra efficiency stage. Sizing its input rating just adds one more division into the efficiency chain:

\[ P_{\text{VSD,input}}=\frac{P_{\text{mechanical load}}}{\eta_{\text{motor}}\eta_{\text{VSD}}} \]

Recall from the Induction Motors page: a VSD achieves smooth starting and speed control via voltage/frequency (V/Hz) control, avoiding the large inrush currents of direct-on-line or even star-delta starting, and can maintain good torque capability across a wide speed range.
