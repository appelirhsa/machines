⚙️ Outcome A: DC Systems & Mechanics

Driving masses/inertias, gearboxes, linear motion (elevators), and DC-motor efficiency chains — the "glue" between DC machine theory and real systems in your past papers.

1. Torque, speed, power & inertia basics

\[ P=T\omega \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi N}{60} \qquad T=J\left(\frac{d\omega}{dt}\right)+b\omega+T_{\text{load}} \]

J is the total moment of inertia (motor rotor + load + couplings, referred to the same shaft), and b is a viscous damping/friction coefficient. At steady speed, dω/dt = 0 and the motor torque simply balances the load (+ friction) torque.

Every rotational formula here has a direct translational twin: F = ma ↔ T = Jα; F = mg (lifting) ↔ weight torque; P = Fv ↔ P = Tω. Elevator/hoist problems are just this translational side dressed up with a drum to convert rotation → linear motion.

2. Gearboxes — the ideal (lossless) picture, then efficiency

An ideal gearbox conserves power: whatever speed is traded away, torque is gained by the same ratio (and vice versa).

\[ n=\frac{N_{\text{in}}}{N_{\text{out}}}=\frac{T_{\text{out}}}{T_{\text{in}}} \]
Ideal, lossless gearbox relation.

For a belt-and-pulley system, the ratio is simply the ratio of pulley diameters (belt speed is equal at both pulleys):

\[ N_1D_1 = N_2D_2 \]

Real gearboxes lose some power to friction, so an efficiency ηgb < 1 must be included. The key exam skill is knowing which side to divide or multiply by η on:

  • Going from output (load) requirement → input (motor) requirement: input torque needed = (ideal input torque) / ηgb, and input power needed = (ideal input power)/ηgb — the motor always has to supply more than the lossless ideal.
  • Going from input (motor) → output (load) delivered: output power delivered = (input power) × ηgb — always less than the lossless ideal.
Watch pulley/gear direction carefully in these problems — "the driving pulley diameter" and "the driven pulley diameter" get swapped in some past-paper wording specifically to test whether you're just plugging numbers in or actually understanding which shaft turns faster.

3. Linear motion — elevators, hoists & vehicles

\[ F=mg \qquad T_{\text{drum}} = Fr_{\text{drum}} \qquad P=Fv \]
For lifting at constant speed (no acceleration).

If there IS acceleration (e.g. the load starts from rest and speeds up), add the inertial term: F = m(g ± a) depending on direction, and refer all inertias to the same shaft via the gear ratio squared when computing effective J.

Worked example

An elevator (no counterweight): cage 1 t, payload 3 t, lifted 30 m in 15 s, drum diameter 1.5 m. Find drum torque, and motor speed/gearbox ratio if the motor turns at 1500 rpm.

  1. Total mass = 4000 kg. F = 4000×9.81 = 39 240 N.
  2. Tdrum = F×r = 39240×0.75 = 29 430 N·m.
  3. Drum speed: v = 30/15 = 2 m/s → ωdrum = v/r = 2/0.75 = 2.667 rad/s → Ndrum = 25.46 rpm.
  4. Gear ratio = Nmotor/Ndrum = 1500/25.46 ≈ 58.9 : 1.

4. DC efficiency chains & battery sizing

Many of your past-paper problems chain several DC-system stages together — a battery feeding a converter feeding a DC motor feeding a gearbox feeding a load (or the reverse, working backwards from a required load power to a required battery current). The rule is always the same: multiply efficiencies going forward (input → output); divide by them going backward (output requirement → input requirement).

\[ P_{\text{battery}}=\frac{P_{\text{load}}}{\eta_{\text{converter}}\eta_{\text{motor}}\eta_{\text{gearbox}}\cdots} \]

Battery sizing (rough, constant-current approximation):

\[ \text{Runtime (hours)} \approx \frac{\text{Capacity (A·h)}}{\text{Discharge current (A)}} \]
Draw the block diagram first (battery → converter → motor → gearbox → load) before writing any equations — almost every mark in these questions is for correctly identifying which efficiency divides which power, and a block diagram makes the direction of the chain visually obvious.

5. Dynamic braking (conceptual — comes up as T/F questions)

Disconnecting a DC motor's armature from the supply and switching a resistor across it lets the stored kinetic energy drive current through that resistor, bringing the machine to rest quickly. It is simple and effective, but the energy is simply dissipated as I²R heat — not recovered. (Compare with regenerative braking, where the machine is run briefly as a generator feeding energy back into the supply — that IS energy-efficient.)
